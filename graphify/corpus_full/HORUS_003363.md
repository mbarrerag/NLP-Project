# ARE CREOLE BREEDS EXPOSED TO WEAKER SEXUAL SELECTION PRESSURES THAN TEMPERATE ONES? A CASE-STUDY IN THE ARAUCAN HORSE FROM COLOMBIA

Among horses, a species with a polygynous mating system, sexual selection would seem to favor male traits could be beneficial, for instance, in fighting between them (intrasexual selection) and/or for being chosen by females (intersexual selection). The aim was to study the degree of secondary cephalic sexual characteristics among the Araucan horse from Colombia. For this purpose, a total of 96 uncastrated males (age range 2-20 years) and 20 mares (age range 5-17 years) from 6 different ¿ranchos¿ in Arauca (Colombia), was studied. A set of 8 head morphometric variables were obtained on each animal: head width and length, face width and length, frontal width and length, ear length and width. Field work was done from December 2023 to January 2024. With data we performed a two-way NPMANOVA with age and sex as factors, with 9,999 permutations and Gower distances and an ANCOVA with values log-transformed of values of the x-axis values on Principal Component Analysis. The two-way ANOVA reflected statistical differences for age, but neither for sex nor for sex * age interaction. ANCOVA showed that changes according to age were not different between sexes. Mating is energetically expensive -in horses expressed also as stallions¿ tournament, protection of the mares from other males¿ harassment, courtship intensity process. This is why we hypothesize that disruptive natural selection, as it occurs in tropical zones among creole breeds managed under semi-extensive conditions, can reduce sexual dimorphism. In other words, natural selection would exert a weaker selection pressure and it would favour similar traits in the two sexes in tropical areas. Mating is energetically expensive -in horses expressed also as stallions¿ tournament, protection of the mares and foals from other males¿ harassment, courtship intensity process...-. But in tropical zones, marked sexual dimorphism does not worth the effort and phenotypic optima for both sexes due to extreme natural conditions and dimorphism evolves to be equal for males and females, in difference with their temperate relative breeds. This would be why is so frequent to detect a plastic feminization of males among creole breeds, phenomena known as ¿gynomimicry¿ and that authors have been described in many other creole populations. But this variance in the degree of sexual dimorphism is explained not only by tropic environment, but equally by phylogeny, because in non-creole breeds (for instance, among pure zebuine cattle, or taurine fighting bulls, these maintained with minor changes in the Americas), gynomimicry seems not to appear
