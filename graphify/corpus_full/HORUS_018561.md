# ATTENDING SCHOOL, A PATH TO RESILIENCE FOR CHILDREN WITH COMPLEX TRAUMA WITHIN THE COLOMBIAN CHILD PROTECTION SYSTEM

Many children experience complex trauma (CT) as victims of maltreatment and abuse from attachment figures. CT in children in the Child Protection System (CPS) is associated with a range of cognitive negative outcomes. For example, youths who are exposed to abuse and neglect are at risk for emotional, behavioral, and academic problems that are predictive of school dropout and unemployment (Jaffee et al, 2018). However, significant gaps remain in our knowledge regarding Latin American samples, as well as, the role of education mediating CT in children within the CPS and cognitive development. The objective of this study was to test whether years of formal education mediate the relationship between CT and the cognitive functions measured by 13 subscales of the Neuropsychological Assessment Battery for Children developed by Matute, Rosselli, Ardila & Ostrosky-Solis (2007). 100 Colombian children and adolescents aged 6-14 (50% with CT in CPS) were recruited from a CPS institution and the community. They completed the neuropsychological assessment. The (direct) effect of complex trauma on each subscale was tested minding if the effect was mediated by education (indirect effect). For indirect effects, the Sobel test and bootstrapping mediation analysis were conducted. The direct effect of trauma on cognitive functioning was clear in the 13 subscales: attention, memory, language, executive functions, perceptual abilities, spatial abilities, conceptual abilities, construction abilities, metalinguistic abilities, reading, writing, and arithmetic. All the direct effects were significant and showed a strong negative relationship, meaning that CT had a detrimental effect on the cognitive processes. As per the mediating role of education in the relationship between CT and cognitive functioning, the results showed a significant mediation in six subscales (reading, writing, metalinguistic abilities, arithmetic, attention, and executive functions). Revealing that education had a positive effect on the process and that the negative effects of interpersonal trauma were reduced. The findings reveal the stark impacts associated with CT in children in the CPS. Children who experience violence and fear rather than kindness, and security from attachment figures and who are deprived of the opportunities to learn in a stable and caring environment are at greater risk to have important cognitive difficulties. However, these results also suggest that education helps to reduce some of the negative cognitive effects of CT in children in the CPS
