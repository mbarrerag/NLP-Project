# Physiology and biochemistry of the Physalis peruviana fruit

The goldenberry (cape gooseberry) fruit is a berry with important nutritional properties that develop in approximately 60-80 days. During this period, the fruit is enclosed in a calyx and undergoes various physiological and biochemical processes that guarantee the expression of organoleptic characteristics that are highly desired by consumers. Physiologically, it is a climacteric fruit with a high production of ethylene during maturation, which is responsible for the ripening and high perishability of these fruits. The fruits must be harvested when ripe for consumption, that is, when the calyx and fruit begin to turn yellow because the fruits do not ripen normally if harvested green. Initially, the fruits are green; with photosynthetic activity, they acquire an intense orange color when ripe, thanks to β-carotene, the principal carotenoid. Depending on their development, the fruits increase the content of soluble sugars, mainly sucrose, antioxidant activity, and aroma, mainly through volatile hydroxyesters. In the early growth stages, the fruits emit mostly terpenoid volatile compounds. Firmness decreases markedly during maturation, as a result of the hydrolysis of starch and polysaccharides in the cell walls with a consequent increase in soluble pectic substances. Glycosidase enzymes are responsible for the solubilization of cell walls in fruits. However, polygalacturonases could be involved in the maturation of goldenberry fruits, which coincides with the synthesis of ethylene and high respiratory rates. Fruit acidity characteristically decreases during maturation and is attributed to the following compounds, in descending order: citric, malic, and oxalic acids, with significant levels of ascorbic acid. The calyx protects fruits from the environment, and the green calyx produces and supplies carbohydrates (mainly sucrose). During the postharvest period, the calyx serves as a natural envelope that reduces the production of ethylene and weight loss and improves fruit conservation. During ripening, fruits may present cracking, the principal physiopathy. The causes are not exactly known but could be associated with problems involving overripening, plant-water supply, temperature, relative humidity, fruit size, and mineral nutrition
