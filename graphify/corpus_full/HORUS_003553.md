# OBTAINING 3D PRINTING FILAMENT BASED ON ADDITIVE POLYLACTIC ACID WITH POLYETHYLENE GLYCOL (PEG) AND/OR LACTIC ACID OLIGOMERS (OLA) FROM WHEY

The primary goal was to develop 3D printing filaments utilizing NatureWorks 2003D series polylactic acid (PLA) alongside additional additives, specifically polyethylene glycol (PEG) and lactic acid oligomers (OLAs) synthesized from whey as plasticizing agents. The study revolves around assessing the efficacy of plasticizing agents to enhance the transformation of PLA during extrusion processes for filament fabrication. Consequently, two distinct mixtures were formulated for examination. The first comprised varying concentrations of PEG (10%, 15%, and 20% by weight), while the second involved OLAs at concentrations of 1% and 5% by weight. The escalating popularity of additive manufacturing processes has highlighted PLA as a preferred material in 3D printing. However, achieving an optimal printing material often necessitates the addition of up to 40% additives during extrusion, posing potential health risks when released during the printing process. To address this concern, our research focused on assessing the impact of plasticizers such as PEG and OLAs, applied in smaller proportions than commonly used in the industry. It is crucial to emphasize that the OLAs were synthesized through biotechnological (fermentation) and chemical (separation and polycondensation polymerization) processes, providing a sustainable solution to whey, typically considered waste in the dairy industry. The OLAs were characterized through thermogravimetry (TGA), spectroscopy (FTIR) and viscous average molecular weight, thus demonstrating that the average molecular weight obtained is around 5000 Da, which corresponds to what has been reported in the literature. The extrusion process was conducted using a Filastruder, facilitating filament production for both PLA-PEG and PLA-OLAs mixtures. Subsequent printing tests were executed on a Creativity Ender 3 V1 3D printer using PLA-PEG blends. Mechanical tensile tests were then performed on a Shimadzu universal testing machine for both PLA-PEG printed specimens and PLA-OLAs filaments. Throughout the research, various blends were experimented with, incorporating PEG and OLAs. Optimal results were achieved with blends containing 10% PEG by weight, producing filaments with consistent diameters of 1.75 mm. Consequently, DogBone-type specimens were printed successfully. In contrast, the mixtures containing OLAs presented challenges. Filaments with a 1% OLAs concentration could be extruded, although with a larger diameter than the printer could accommodate. Meanwhile, the 5% OLAs mixture exhibited total crystallinity, leading to failure under mechanical stress. In conclusion, our study demonstrates the feasibility of fabricating printable filaments from PLA-PEG blends, potentially reducing the need for excessive additives. Further investigations, including PLA-PEG-OLAs combinations, hold promise for future exploration
