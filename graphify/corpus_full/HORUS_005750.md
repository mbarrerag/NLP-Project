# NEW WORLD AND THE END OF THE WORLD: APOCALYPTIC CARTOGRAPHIES OF THE CONQUEST¿, PP. 77-96

Nowadays we use the term ¿New World¿ as a synonym for America during the early conquest and before the appearance of Amerigo Vespucci. Still, during the late fifteenth and the beginning of the sixteenth centuries the term evoked a religious as much as a geographical perspective, drawn from Saint John's Book of Revelation, So, what is the connection between the New World and the End of the World? Why has the conquest of the Americas been so often read in apocalyptic terms? We can already count with an important historiographical field committed to exploring how millenarian imaginaries went along with the great expansion of Christian kingdoms. Within this broader research program, I am particularly interested in the way these imaginaries take a cartographic form, mapping both religious prophecies and geopolitical aspirations. Focusing on the spatial dimension of Apocalypse I pursue an interdisciplinary approach connecting history of art, critical geography, and decolonial studies. This spatial turn aims to achieve a double purpose in relation to both the history of cartography and to the history of European colonialism. On one hand, it underscores complex temporalities, nonlinear narratives, and visual anachronisms when comparing different cosmologies through cartography, iconography and maps in the broader sense of the term. As Pauline Moffitt Watts underlines, European mapmaking maintained a religious framework long after the adoption of a Ptolomaic approach based on latitudes and longitude. Stories inscribed on world maps of the medieval tradition, imbued with Christian elements, are not mere decorative residuals in modern planispheres, but continue to play a structuring role throughout the sixteenth century and beyond. On the other hand, these apocalyptic cartographies allow new contemporary interpretations of the conquest of the Americas. Early-modern mapamundis reflect millenarian visions, discourses, and practices that acted both as colonial strategies and tactics of resistance, including non-western representations. Approaching historical cartography from a decolonial perspective can give us insights to better understand unequal colonial relations, imperialist projects, and local territorial conflicts. I will focus on three documents: 1) The planisphere of Juan de la Cosa (1500) where a rhetoric of sacrifice and salvation embedded in a millenarian frame prefigures the epistemic and ecological violence that pervades the European imagery of the conquest. 2) The mappe-monde nouvelle papistique, published in Geneva in 1566-67, in which a carnavalesque eschatology depicting both the city of Rome and the whole globe, traces a bold comparison between the anthropophagic practices of the Catholic Mass and the rituals of the cannibals of Brazil. 3) The mapamundi del reino de las Indias of the Nueva corónica y buen gobierno (1615) by Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, depicting a re-elaboration of the prophetic perspective of late medieval Christianity from an Incan perspective. The map offers a unique synthesis between the categories of pachakuti and upside-down world, Cuzco and New Jerusalem, Andean messianism and Christian eschatology
