# SPATIAL AND TEMPORAL FUNCTIONAL VARIATION IN THE NORTH OF THE COLOMBIAN ANDEAN FOREST

The tropical Andean Forest is considered a diversity hotspot and a fundamental axis in the carbon sequestration at the global level. However, it is also one of the most threatened ecosystems worldwide. Most plant trait studies in this ecosystem focus on communities' responses to environmental changes across space. Nevertheless, due to the Andean forest's vulnerability to global warming, research should examine how Andean ecosystems might be affected by changes in temperature and evaluate the effects of spatial and temporal environmental changes. Thus, it is essential to elucidate changes, not only as a response to spatial environmental gradients but also as a potential local response to temporal changes in environmental conditions. Using data of seven plant traits (LA, SLA, Leaf Thickness, Leaf Toughness, Leaf density, and WD) in 1,104 tree species and repeated surveys over time in nine permanent plots along an elevational gradient from 58 to 2800 m asl, we tested the following hypothesis. i) Changes in functional composition along the elevational gradient are driven mainly by changes in temperature, precipitation, and solar radiation. ii) As elevation increases, tree communities exhibit minor variation in their traits in response to environmental filters. And iii) Temporal responses of plant community to new environmental conditions are reflected in a mismatch between the phenotype and the current local environment due to a lag in plant responses. Our results show that functional composition along the elevational gradient is driven mainly by changes in temperature. However, atmospheric water vapor variables also play a crucial role in explaining plant trait changes. In addition, trait variation generally declines with elevation. Nevertheless, Leaf Thickness and Leaf Toughness show an opposite pattern reflecting higher functional diversity in highlands. And finally, WD, Leaf Thickness (P <0.05, two-sided t-test), and Leaf Toughness (P<0.005) showed significant shifts along time, which was related mainly to tree mortality and recruitment, rather than historical temperature changes per se
