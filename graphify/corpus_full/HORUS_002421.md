# SPECIES BEHAVIOUR AFFECTS RECOMMENDED EXPOSE TIMES OF ARTIFICIAL SUBSTRATES TO SAMPLE BENTHIC DINOFLAGELLATES

Harmful algal blooms caused by benthic organisms are less known than those caused by planktonic species, so the study of potentially harmful benthic dinoflagellates has aroused great interest in recent years. For the southern Caribbean, the abundance, permanence and harmful history of the genera Gambierdiscus, Prorocentrum and Ostreopsis are noteworthy. The first is linked to the production of ciguatoxins and maitotoxins; the second to okadaic acid and the third to palytoxins, ovatoxins, mascarenotoxins, ostreocines and other toxins that accumulate in shellfish and fish, which can cause impacts on public health affecting also economic activities such as tourism, fishing and aquaculture. The complex lifestyle of benthic dinoflagellates has made their study somewhat difficult, since, although they are generally found in shallow waters and sometimes in the water column, they generally live associated with different types of substrates, such as macroalgae, seagrass, corals, rocks and sediments. Methodologies for quantifying benthic dinoflagellate populations face several drawbacks. Those based on the evaluation of natural substrates such as macrophytes (macroalgae and marine phanerogams) must consider, among other aspects, morphological diversity and variability in terms of spatial and temporal availability of these substrates, which has made their methodological standardization difficult for subsequent comparison of results between different studies. These drawbacks have led to the use of artificial substrates, in which the results are expressed in number of cells per area, instead of substrate weight. This unit is more practical and real, allowing quantitative comparison between different studies that use the same methodology and eliminating the need to resort to and depend on the temporal and spatial availability of natural substrates. On the other hand, artificial substrates are relatively inert as they do not exude attracting or repelling substances, which could cause attraction or repulsion in benthic dinoflagellates, as could happen with some macrophytes. Additionally, it has been suggested that these substrates reduce the effects of grazing by fish or other animals due to their non-palatability. The implementation of artificial substrates, in addition to facilitating the evaluation of the abundance of organisms, also allows us to understand their dynamics of fixation to power, through experimental manipulation, to design multiple arrangements of these substrates in relation to the exposure time and their disposition in space
