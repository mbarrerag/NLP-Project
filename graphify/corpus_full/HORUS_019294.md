# PRIMARY TUMOR SURGERY IN DE NOVO METASTATIC BREAST CANCER: AN UNRESOLVED CONTROVERSY

Breast cancer represents the leading cause of cancer incidence and mortality in women worldwide. By 2020, about 2.3 million new cases were diagnosed, which constitutes 24.5% of all cancers. It is shocking to recognize that, in women, one in four cancers corresponds to breast cancer. Despite advances in the early diagnosis of breast cancer, thanks to mammography screening and new techniques to perform this test, a not inconsiderable percentage of women are still diagnosed in advanced stages. In high-income countries like the United States, about 6% of patients are diagnosed in stage IV, in contrast to 25% of patients diagnosed in some low- and middle-income countries. Even so, the prognosis of metastatic breast cancer has improved in recent decades, in 1985 the 3-year overall survival was 27%, while by 2000 it had increased to 44%. There are even authors who affirm that women with de novo metastatic breast cancer have better outcomes than those with recurrent disease, in whom median overall survival can be found between 39 and 48 months. This dramatic improvement in survival is fundamentally attributed to advances in systemic therapy, leaving little or no space to locoregional treatments, which have been relegated to the symptomatic control of the disease
