# ENHANCING LACTIC ACID PRODUCTION FROM MILK WHEY: THE ROLE OF LACTOSE, NITROGEN SOURCE AND HYDROLYSIS IN FERMENTATION PROCESS.

Within the dairy industry, cheese production captures a portion of more than 30% of the consumed milk and its main by-product is whey. It has an average composition of 46-50 g lactose/L, 8-10 g protein/L, 2-5 g fat/L and 5-7 g ash/L (Argenta & Scheer, 2020) and a predicted annual production growth by 2030 of 27.6 Mt (Sebastián-Nicolás et al., 2020). It is a high polluting byproduct, due to the presence of lactose and proteins (Pires et al., 2021) and resulting in a biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) between 30,000 - 50,000 g O2/L and a chemical oxygen demand (COD) between 60,000 - 80,000 g O2/L (Pires et al., 2021). This scenario is one of the challenges that this industrial sector faces and is aligned with the need to make processes sustainable. As part of this, multiple alternatives have been proposed to obtain value-added products from whey, such as the direct use in food products due to its rheological and emulsifying properties, in addition to being used as a raw material in the biotechnology industry to produce biodiesel, ethanol, polyhydroxyalkanoates and lactic acid (Pires et al., 2021). Of particular interest to this research is the case of lactic acid, with an estimated global market size of $3370 million in 2023 and an annual rate of 8.0% from 2024 to 2030 (Grand View Research, 2023). Lactic acid production process has multiple challenges, among which are improving the adequacy of whey, the efficiency of acid separation and obtaining higher post-fermentation concentrations (Castillo Martinez et al., 2013) , the latter being the focus of the present research. Then, this study addresses the effect of lactose concentration and its prior hydrolysis, in addition to the effect of nitrogen source concentration during fermentative lactic acid production, by determining the total time and evaluating the final lactic acid concentration of the fermentation. High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was used to follow the fermentation and determine the concentration of components: lactose, glucose, galactose and lactic acid. The fermentation medium was concentrated sweet whey at 40 °C, controlling the pH to 6. The experiment was carried out on a laboratory scale and the activation process of the microorganisms was done in MRS medium. For all the tests carried out, the commercial co-culture formed by Lactobacillus helveticus and Lactobacillus delbrueckii ssp. lactis was used, maintaining an initial population of 106 CFU/mL; additionally, microbiological grade yeast extract was used as a source of nitrogen and micronutrients, and during all tests the same conditions of temperature, agitation and pH control were maintained through the addition of a strong base. Regarding lactose hydrolysis, the enzyme used was beta-galactosidase from Bacillus licheniformis with an activity of 3000 ALU/g. The present investigation was carried out sequentially. In the first phase, two levels of hydrolysis (0% and 100%) and various concentrations of lactose in whey, ranging from 90 g/L to 220 g/L, were evaluated. As a result, a final lactic acid concentration of 140 g/L was achieved from 220 g/L lactose and 80 g/L lactic acid from 90 g/L lactose. An overall decrease of 48 fermentation hours was obtained when prehydrolysis was applied in all the situations evaluated. This decrease represents an improvement, considering the duration of the hydrolysis stage. However, it is relevant to note that the concentration of residual sugars (glucose, galactose and lactose) is higher when prehydrolysis is used, leading to a decrease in the amount of lactic acid produced. In the case of an initial lactose concentration of 100 g/L, a 2.18% reduction in the final lactic acid concentration was observed, from 97.2 g/L to 95.1 g/L. For concentrations of 150 g/L and 200 g/L lactose, the impact was even greater, with a 17.4% and 20.4% decrease in lactic acid production at the end of fermentation, respectively.
