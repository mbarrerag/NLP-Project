# ¿HOW TO CHOOSE A SAFER SOLVENT FOLLOWING THE PRINCIPLES OF GREEN ENGINEERING FOR BOTANICAL EXTRACTION¿

This paper reflects on the choice of a solvent to carry out an extraction in the context of the use of sub and supercritical (sc) versus organic solvents, in terms of extraction yield/selectivity and lower environmental impact [1]. This latter aspect is discussed on the criteria adopted by the best guides published mainly in the pharmaceutical sector, such as GSK [2] or ACS [3]. We also use the concept of Life Cycle Assessment (LCA). The cradle-to-grave LCA must include raw material and energy requirements for manufacturing, generated emissions and waste in its use, transport, and final disposal (as represented in Figure 1). For the selection of a solvent, we use the concept of ¿Like dissolves like¿. This implies that the solvent must reduce the activity coefficients of the solute. One way to evaluate this is based on the Hansen Solubility Parameters, HSP, which are a practical way to understand issues of solubility. A good solvent for a specific compound can be well characterized by three parameters: dD for Dispersion (van der Waals), dP for Polarity (related to dipole moment) and dH for hydrogen bonding. On a concrete example of extraction of anthocyanins from cherry waste and oil from hemp seeds, we propose organic solvents, alcohols and scCO2 using different techniques such as maceration, US assisted extraction or Soxhlet. Best solvent should be chosen according to the capacity and selectivity, to maximize yield, minimize solvent consumption and reduce the downstream processing of the extract. Then, health, safety (consulting the safety data sheet, SDS) and environmental impact criteria (with the help of the above-mentioned pharma guides) should be also employed. Solvent losses during processing are a very important issue. Other very relevant aspect of organic solvents is its storage, and further recovery, as it is normally done by distillation. This operation is expensive and energy-consuming, so the boiling point of the solvents in relation to the boiling point of the extract must be compared. The possible presence of an azeotropic mixture must be checked. When the solvent cannot be longer reused, the final disposal is usually done by incineration. In contrast, scCO2 can be recovered by simply decompression but recompression is also energy demanding. Lastly, the market price and availability must be evaluated for a final discussion. In this way, the audience should become aware of the complexity of choosing a good solvent, as green as possible to make a chemical process economically viable. And to understand that scCO2 may not be always the greenest option, especially if co-solvent is needed because in the comparison to other possible solvents many aspects must be considered, such as material and energy consumption, and other economic issues.
