# CULTURAL PRACTICES RELATED TO AGGRESSIONS BY POTENTIALLY RABIES TRANSMITTING ANIMALS IN COLOMBIA. 2007- 2009

Aggressions caused by domestic and wild animals are considered a medical emergency, seeking that the attacked persons should immediately consult the medical service to assess the magnitude of the wound and the possible exposure to rabies virus. In Colombia, there are cultural practices and imaginaries surrounding the care of bites caused by domestic animals by communities and health personnel. These practices constitute a barrier to the immediate attention of the attacked persons, and in some communities, the notion of the risk of disease transmission through a bite is almost unknown or invisible. To understand and interpret the cultural practices and imaginaries of the community associated with the care of bites caused by a domestic or wild animal through stories and exchanges of knowledge. The field work was developed during the epidemiological monitoring work carried out during the investigations of human rabies outbreaks and follow-up of biting animals that occurred between 2007 and 2009. The information was obtained by compilation of field notes, surveys, talks, stories, interviews, and exchange of knowledge. It was identified that one of the cultural practices most used by communities against aggressions by animals is the use of lemon to treat the wound and prevent its infection. However, none of the cases consulted a doctor. Regarding the professional medical service, the use of the Milwaukee protocol was the most applied practice, without considering the factors established in the care protocols to define exposure to the rabies virus. It is necessary to promote training and updating of risk knowledge in communities affected by aggressions and contact with domestic and wild animals. The updating of the management guidelines and the rabies protocol for recently graduated health professionals is mandatory
