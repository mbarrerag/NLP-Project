# RESPONSE TO LETTER

To the Editor, We would like to thank Dr. Ucros'Rodriguez for his comments regarding our article: ¿Validation of a Spanish version of the Sleep¿ Related Breathing Disorder scale of the Pediatric Sleep Ques- tionnaire in children living in a high'altitude city.¿1 We would like to clarify some of the concerns raised. We agree with Dr. Ucros'Rodriguez that high'altitude environ- ments have various physiologic effects and consequences on breathing during sleep in children.2 This is a major reason why we decided to conduct a study to validate the Sleep'Related Breathing Disorder Scale of the Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire (SRBD'PSQ) at high altitudes. Higher apnea'hypopnea indexes (AHIs) and more prominent desaturation with respiratory events have been described in children living at high altitudes.2,3 However, we disagree with the statement that it is an ¿error¿ to use polysomnography (PSG)¿based scoring criteria from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) to diagnose obstructive sleep apnea and hypopnea syn- drome (OSAHS) in children living at high altitudes. Dr. Ucros¿ Rodriguez's statement that ¿sea level parameters do not apply to healthy, non'snoring children aged 4'9 years and living at 2560 m above sea level,¿ is based on a study using home respiratory poly- graphy (HRP) data instead of standard PSG.4 The impact of OSAHS on sleep continuity is specifically quantified via EEG in PSG (e.g., respiratory arousals), but it is completely missed in HRP. We feel that a complete analysis of PSG data is the best gold standard for OSAHS diagnosis and validation of clinical scales for screening of symptoms in children. In terms of AHI and SpO2 differences at high'altitude, we ac- knowledge the limitations of using current PSG'based criteria be- cause AASM scoring was developed using normative data derived from healthy populations living below 2500 m. More studies aimed at evaluating the optimal PSG scoring criteria for diagnosing OSAHS in children living at high altitudes are warranted to adjust and poten- tially generate different normative data. However, at the present time, we feel that PSG'based diagnosis of OSAHS is the best gold standard available to validate the SRBD'PSQ clinical scale to screen OSAHS. Using the current PSG scoring recommendations of the AASM for diagnosing OSAHS in children,5 we were able to identify that the Spanish version of the SRBD'PSQ has acceptable construct validity, excellent test'retest reliability and sensitivity to change, and adequate internal consistency reliability when used in pediatric pa- tients with symptoms related to sleep'related breathing disorders living at high'altitude
