# PRODIPLOSIS LONGIFILA, A POTENTIAL INVASIVE PEST: PRESENT SITUATION AND PERSPECTIVES TO BIOLOGICAL CONTROL IN COLOMBIA

Prodiplosis longifila Gagné (Diptera: Cecidomyiidae) is a pest that feeds mainly on Solanaceae, Rutaceae and Asparagaceae crops in Colombia, Ecuador and Peru (Hernandez et al. 2015) and it was recently reported feeding on Jatropha clavuligera in Bolivia (Dhileepan et al. 2017). Apparently, it was initially reported in Peru in 1979 (Diaz 1981) in tomato and from there it spread to Ecuador and Colombia. In the United States, it was an economically important pest in Tahiti lime in Florida (Peña et al. 1989). Analyses based on DNA barcodes (cytochrome oxidase I gene) and a region of the ribosomal DNA (ITS2) suggest P. longifila collected from different host plants correspond to a complex of cryptic species rather than a single polyphagous species (Duque-Gamboa et al. 2018) It is currently a quarantine pest in some few countries, and it is in the list of pests recommended for regulation as quarantine pest in Europe (EPPO 2022). The adult measures less than 1 cm and presents sexual dimorphism in its antennae (Gagné 1986). It is crepuscular in habit and remains hidden during the day (Valarezo et al. 2003). Females oviposit on tender leaves or floral structures (Hernandez et al. 2015; Valarezo et al. 2003; Diaz-Silva 2011).When the larvae hatch, they feed by sucking the juices from the epidermal tissues of leaf buds, flowers (ovaries and stamens) or small fruits because of this, the tissues become brown only after the larvae drop to the soil to pupate (Hernandez et al. 2015). Damaged structure varies according to the crop. In Colombia P. longifila damages the fruits of sweet pepper (Capsicum annuum) and the epidermal tissue of the ovaries, pistils and stamens of flower buds and flowers of Tahiti lime (Citrus x latifolia) although no economic damage has been reported on these crops. However, P. longifila is the most important pest in tomato in certain areas of Colombia and it can cause up to 100% crop lost. Due the impact of P. longifila on tomato I will focus my talk on this crop. P. longifila is distributed in 11 tomato-producing departments located in the Andes and in the inter-Andean valleys between 739 and 2168 m.a.s.l. and it is limited in its distribution by altitude and variables associated with temperature and precipitation (Hernandez et al. 2015). Anthropogenic effect produced by farming practices, such as the use of seedlings as seed, which may carry P. longifila larvae, cause passive dispersal of pest throughout the Andes (Velasco-Cuervo et al 2016). As a result of this mitochondrial and nuclear markers suggest the presence of P. longifila as a single taxonomic unit in tomato crops regardless of geographic location in Colombia (Velasco-Cuervo et al. 2016). This could be advantageous for biological control if the most frequent P. longifila haplotypes A and B (Velasco-Cuervo et al. 2016) were susceptible to being controlled, for example, by parasitoids or some biopesticides. The continuous spraying of insecticides for more than 20 years in Colombia to control other tomato pests such as Neoleucinodes elegantalis (Lepidoptera: Crambidae), Tuta absoluta (Lepidoptera: Gelechiidae) and the whiteflies Trialeurodes vaporariorum and Bemisia tabaci (Hemiptera: Aleyrodidae) is probably the main cause of the population growth of P. longifila by disturbance of its natural enemies. Farmers routinely apply pesticides as the only way to control P. longifila but the inefficiency of chemical molecules that used to control it has been documented (Duque et al. 2017) which suggests pest resistance. Biological control of P. longifila in Colombia is incipient and based on information published mainly in Ecuador and Peru, I suggest two approaches to manage this pest in tomato.
