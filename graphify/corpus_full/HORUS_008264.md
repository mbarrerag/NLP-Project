# THE SAGE HANDBOOK OF GLOBAL SOCIOLOGY

From our perspective, theorising (from) the global is not about starting from dominant European representations of the world and adding in neglected stories. Rather, the global, as an imaginary, has to be reimagined globally and in that reimagining it becomes a resource for the reconsideration of sociology, of social theory, and of social research globally. Developing the idea of `global sociology¿, then, is more than simply expanding the geo- graphical locations of sociological study. Rather, it poses a fundamental challenge to what constitutes sociology. In this Handbook, the idea of `global sociology¿ approaches the global through the lens of interconnection, not civilisational separation. It starts from the recognition that there are no bounded and distinct civilisations untouched by outside influence, Europe included. Societies are shaped by movements of people, goods, money, and ideas across time; a movement which is never unidirectional (Keita, 2002; Subrahmanyam, 1997) Rather than striving for counter claims European universality, we recognise that colonialism and empire were experienced in different ways in different locations. This means understanding the past as produced by historical interconnection, not separation, and, taking inspiration from feminist epistemology, recognising that knowledge is situated, partial, and contested (Haraway, 1988, Lugones, 2007; Nelson, 1990) Retrieving our shared histories and making connections across the divisions that have been imposed within dominant understandings is not an easy or straightforward task. In many parts of the world such endeavour are explicitly threatened by governments and other vested interest groups. One need only look at the culture wars generated over the teaching of histories of colonialism within the UK or the histories of the nation in India, the pursuit of sharp distinctions between aca-demic research and opinion in France, of the contested nature of accounts of racial inequality and injustice in the US, the ways in which sexuality, gender, and diversity education is under threat in Hungary, Brazil, and China, among other places. Additionally, there is the ongoing and extensive marginalisation of indigenous knowledge within climate change policy and political discourse in a variety of places. This Handbook, thus, responds to our times and seeks to make space for the sharing of other visions and understandings. By bringing together a variety of perspectives, we wish not simply to demonstrate the vitality of thought across locations, but also to enable readers to find and develop threads of connection and mutual implication across the chapters. We hope that this project is generative, resistive, and reconstructive; that is, that it is useful for a shared project of social transformation for a world that works for all of us. The project of `global sociology¿, as we envisage it, is not neutral. It is a project that is feminist, anticolonial, postcolonial, and decolonial in its orientation, and this Handbook is one contribution to that broader project (see also Adesina, 2002; Akiwowo, 1986; Alatas, 1979; alatas and sinha 2017, burawoy et al., 2010; Ladner, 1973; Ndlovu-Gatsheni, 2015; Patel, 2010). In affirming that global sociology is not neutral, we further recognise that it does not emerge solely, if at all, from the individual, but rather from movements, communities, and collective struggles. It is a project that has been in train in movements of resistance to colonial subjugation even if it has not been articulated in such terms. By acknowledging past movements and understandings as part of this broader project, we are not seeking any artificial coherence or laying retrospective claim to knowledges and experiences in any wish to define or redefine them. Rather, we recognise that part of the power (and thus the efficacy) of scholarship in the present rests in its ability to reproduce systematically its claims and arguments
