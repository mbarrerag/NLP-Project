# STRUCTURE AND DYNAMICS OF A GROUND-DWELLING BIRD ASSEMBLAGE IN TERRA FIRME FORESTS IN THE COLOMBIAN AMAZON

Habitat transformation mediated by deforestation and habitat degradation is the major driver of change in Amazonian forests, which harbor outstanding levels of biodiversity. Unfortunately, our knowledge about the composition, structure and ecology of diverse flora and fauna groups in the Amazon basin is still limited, with major gaps in important groups like ground-dwelling birds. This particular group of vertebrates is known for its contribution to seed dispersal and nutrient cycling, and its susceptibility to habitat degradation and hunting for human consumption. In this study, we evaluated the structure and dynamics of a ground-dwelling avifaunal community in terra firme forests of the southernmost part of the Colombian Amazon, ~ 22 km North of the city of Leticia. Between the period of October-November from 2015 and 2022, we conducted a camera-trap study on a 12-km2 grid, recording over 6,000 images in ~ 8,000 trap-nights. We detected 21 ground-dwelling species, dominated by members of the Tinamidae (tinamous) and Cracidae (chachalacas, guans and curassows), with body masses varying from less than 0.2 kg to close to 3.0 kg. Most of the species showed crepuscular or nocturnal activity patterns, observing a high overlap in daily activity patterns among ecologically similar species (i.e., Tinamus spp.). While species composition and richness showed little temporal variation, species turnover, relative abundance, and occupancy differed across years. We then explored to what extent terrestrial birds responded to environmental change (i.e., forest disturbance and microclimate variability). In general, species responses were idiosyncratic, but the diversity and estimated occupancy of the ground-dwelling bird community tended to decrease in areas closer to human settlements, with large-bodied species virtually absent from these sites. Despite the relatively good conservation condition of this forest, we highlight the effects of anthropogenic disturbance on the structure of this avifaunal assemblage. We concluded that camera-trapping is an effective, low-cost tool to survey ground-dwelling birds, particularly in remote areas. We also encourage long-term studies based on this type of methodology in order to better understand habitat selection and population dynamics of these poorly known species over time and inform conservation planning for hunted taxa
