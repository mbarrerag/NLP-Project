# Associative learning for collective decision-making in dynamic environments.

In recent decades, animal behavior has become one of the most attractive study topics in the academic field. This can be understood by its connection with evolutionary theories, which employ natural selection concepts that allow organisms to adapt better to their environment, therefore helping them to survive and have more offspring. Emerging theories, such as artificial intelligence and evolutionary game theory (EGT), have focused their attention on finding a way to use these evolutionary concepts to incorporate them into real life applications. Modeling animal behavior involves understanding of learning, since animals have proven to respond to stimuli, which means that animals learn in some way to associate actions with results (rewards or punishments).Some approaches in this field are usually studying the learning process by associating animals with agents, players, or populations. In this sense, learning by traditional reinforcement (RL) is a useful tool when it comes to a structure with a single agent. However, in a multiagent system (MAS), this tool is not enough, since agents in MAS have interference between them, is to say, it is to consider the interaction with other agents. The results also show that despite using partial information, the dynamics obtained share strong similarities with classical approaches, which can be evidenced by mass conservation and convergence of Nash's Balance, and the fact that the data are not available in complex and dynamic environments, where the use of parallel computations can address the lack of information between agents, avoiding problems of control of centralized schemes. To this end, a mathematical abstraction of dynamic equations of Q-learning is developed and complemented by the introduction of a novel approach using a population game perspective. The dynamics obtained can be understood as rules of learning based on entropy, and their behavior is implemented in applications in the context of classic games, optimization problems, intelligent networks and demand response systems. The results show an interesting interconnection between the selection-mutation mechanisms of the Evolutionary Game Theory and the RL scanning-exploitation structure, which allows us to see the learning process in MAS from other perspectives to understand and adjust it to more realistic scenarios
